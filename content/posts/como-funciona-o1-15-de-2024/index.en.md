## A first look at o1

On **September 12**, OpenAI [released its new LLM, o1](https://x.com/OpenAI/status/1834278217626317026). It was not the expected GPT-5, but a model based on a very different approach, one that [“thinks” before answering](https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/).

From the point of view of a ChatGPT user, the most obvious change is not in the interface but in the rhythm of interaction. You type your prompt, press send, and instead of getting an answer immediately, the model starts **“thinking”**. The OpenAI icon pulses, a “Thinking…” label appears, and the system shows short summaries of what seem to be intermediate reasoning steps.

![](Pasted%20image%2020240924123523.png)

The final answer usually arrives after a noticeable delay, and it tends to be much more elaborate than the answer one would get from GPT-4o. My subjective experience at the time was that, as a programming assistant, o1 felt excellent. It handled complex code and difficult problems with more consistency than previous models.

From the user side, the behavior suggested four immediate conclusions:

- The model generates an internal reasoning process with multiple steps.
- That process is monitored, and only a summary is shown to the user.
- It uses much more time than previous models.
- The answers feel more deliberate and better argued.

## The first evaluations

Early evaluations suggested that this was not just a UX trick. On the [Chatbot Arena leaderboard](https://lmarena.ai/), **o1-preview** quickly climbed to the top, ahead of Gemini 1.5, Grok 2, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet.

There were also striking results on planning-style benchmarks. In particular, **Subbarao Kambhampati** published [a paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13373) on a blocks-world benchmark designed to test the **ability of LLMs to plan actions**, and the jump was dramatic.

![](Pasted%20image%2020240925122527.png)

![](Pasted%20image%2020240925123819.png)

Kambhampati, not someone given to hype, ended up summarizing the change with a phrase I found extremely revealing:

> o1 seems to move beyond the approximate retrieval nature of LLMs toward something like approximate reasoning.

That phrase, **approximate reasoning**, matters a great deal, especially coming from someone who had long argued, correctly, that earlier LLMs could not really plan.

## What OpenAI explains

OpenAI has become increasingly reluctant to explain its models in detail. In the case of o1, the main public material consists of the [post *Learning to Reason with LLMs*](https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/), a list of [contributors to o1](https://openai.com/openai-o1-contributions/), a short [o1-mini post](https://openai.com/index/openai-o1-mini-advancing-cost-efficient-reasoning/), and the [o1 system card](https://assets.ctfassets.net/kftzwdyauwt9/67qJD51Aur3eIc96iOfeOP/71551c3d223cd97e591aa89567306912/o1_system_card.pdf).

From those documents and talks, I took away three main ideas.

### 1. Reinforcement learning with chain-of-thought

OpenAI's first paragraph talks about **“Reinforcement Learning with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning.”**

Chain-of-thought prompting means asking a model to **reason step by step** before producing a final answer. The classic result is that many difficult tasks become far more solvable if the model first writes out intermediate reasoning steps.

![](Pasted%20image%2020240926172506.png)

The other ingredient is **reinforcement learning**, which had already shown its power in systems such as AlphaGo and AlphaZero. The core idea, at least at a high level, is to learn which actions are best in which states by exploring the space of possibilities and rewarding better outcomes.

Applying that to LLMs is nontrivial, because the “state of the world” is now a textual description, often partially generated by the model itself. OpenAI does not explain the training procedure in detail. But **Trapit Bansal** says something important in the interview: instead of relying only on humans to write out reasoning traces, they discovered that if the model is trained with reinforcement learning to **generate and refine its own reasoning chains**, it can outperform human-written chains and, crucially, the process can scale.

That suggests an important shift: o1 is not just trained to predict the next token of ordinary text, but also to predict the next token in a **reasoning trace**.

### 2. Hidden reasoning

The model's reasoning chains are mostly hidden from the user. During reasoning, the interface displays only a summary of what the model is supposedly doing, while the underlying traces remain inaccessible and can be monitored for safety.

The system card frames this as a positive safety feature:

> In addition to monitoring model outputs, we have long studied the possibility of monitoring their latent thinking. Until now, that latent thinking was available only in the form of activations: large blocks of unreadable numbers from which we could extract only simple concepts. Chains of thought are much more legible by default and could allow us to monitor our models for much more complex behaviors.

OpenAI does not explain what model performs that monitoring, whether it is another model such as GPT-4o or o1 itself, but the idea is clear: hidden reasoning is both a capability feature and a safety feature.

### 3. Better results with more computation

One of the most important plots in OpenAI's post is the one showing that **the same model performs better when given more inference-time computation**.

![](compute.png.webp)

The left-hand side is familiar: more training compute improves the model. The right-hand side is the real novelty: a fixed model can be tuned to spend more time thinking, and the longer it thinks, the better it performs. In the plot, the model can move from roughly 20% to 80% on American Math Olympiad questions if given two orders of magnitude more compute.

What is that extra compute being spent on? The simplest answer is: **generating many candidate reasoning chains and many candidate answers**, then selecting the best final answer from that larger search process.

This is also the interpretation that lines up with a slide shown by [Denny Zhou](https://x.com/denny_zhou) in his talk on LLM reasoning.

![](df877c73-075b-4b31-89e8-223e7a0d25e9_1722x368.heic)

The important idea is not the exact formula, but the principle behind it: to get a better answer, you may need to **sample many reasoning paths and keep the best one**. More compute means more search, and more search means better answers.

## The future

What does all this say about the future of LLMs?

The traditional “predict the next word” paradigm may have been only the first step, a bootstrapping stage that allowed us to build more advanced systems. Once strong base models exist, such as GPT-4o mini, they can be used not only to generate training data but also to create richer evaluation functions that go beyond checking whether the next token is correct.

At the same time, we should not underestimate the sheer power of next-token prediction itself. As [Karpathy recently wrote](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1835024197506187617), a great many problems may ultimately be reducible to next-token prediction once the tokenization is chosen appropriately.

> As the LLM stack becomes more mature, we may see a convergence of many different problems into this paradigm. The problem gets reduced to next-token prediction with an LLM; what changes is only the use or meaning of the tokens in each domain.

In other words, the “L” in LLM may turn out to be a temporary historical label. If token sequences can stand for almost anything, then these models may be able to learn and generalize over many domains beyond text, including [chaotic systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15771) or even [protein structure](https://arstechnica.com/science/2023/03/large-language-models-also-work-for-protein-structures/).

![](GYV0RMoWcAAYQ4q.jpeg)

So my conclusion at that point was simple: it still looked far too early to call the end of the curve. For the next few years, I expected continued improvement in reliability and capability, and a gradual approach toward [AGI](/posts/agi-o-no-agi-14-de-2024/).

My closing advice, in three words, Altman-style, was: **don't sell NVIDIA**.
