<p>On <strong>June 10</strong>, Apple held the conference keynote and introduced the new versions of its operating systems: iOS 18 (<a href="https://www.apple.com/ios/ios-18-preview/">preview</a> and <a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/ios-18-makes-iphone-more-personal-capable-and-intelligent-than-ever/">press release</a>), iPadOS (<a href="https://www.apple.com/ipados/ipados-18-preview/">preview</a> and <a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/ipados-18-introduces-powerful-intelligence-features-and-apps-for-apple-pencil/">press release</a>), macOS Sequoia (<a href="https://www.apple.com/macos/macos-sequoia-preview/">preview</a> and <a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/macos-sequoia-takes-productivity-and-intelligence-on-mac-to-new-heights/">press release</a>), watchOS 11 (<a href="https://www.apple.com/watchos/watchos-preview/">preview</a> and <a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/watchos-11-brings-powerful-health-and-fitness-insights/">press release</a>), visionOS 2 (<a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/visionos-2-brings-new-spatial-computing-experiences-to-apple-vision-pro/">press release</a>), and tvOS 18 (<a href="https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2024/06/updates-to-the-home-experience-elevate-entertainment-and-bring-more-convenience/">press release</a>). <p>Personally, what I value most about WWDC is that it showcases the work done by <strong>hundreds of Apple engineers</strong> over the course of a year. That may be an occupational bias from being a software engineer, or perhaps the perspective of a father with daughters in their twenties who are just starting their careers, but when I see all these materials I mostly think about the young people who built them, and about <strong>their effort and their hopes</strong>.</p>

<p>2. One of the announcements I was most curious about was Apple's stance on AI. In the <a href="/en/posts/del-16-al-31-de-mayo-10-de-2024/">previous issue</a> I also listed the questions I expected the conference to answer.</p>

<p>The answers arrived, and so did a certain amount of confusion. Of all the announcements, the one that had the biggest impact was the one Apple least wanted to emphasize: <strong>the deal with OpenAI</strong>. Apple wanted the focus to be on its own work and its own features, but the agreement with <strong>Sam Altman's</strong> company immediately captured media attention and somewhat overshadowed the main message.</p>

<p>However, in the days that followed, as specialized media paid more attention to the features Apple had presented, the company's real idea started to sink in. The marketing team's masterstroke worked very well: the wordplay of turning AI, Artificial Intelligence, into <em><strong>Apple Intelligence</strong></em>.</p>

<p>One of the core ideas behind Apple Intelligence is to <strong>provide concrete features</strong> powered by generative models built by Apple itself, both text models, LLMs, and image models. Apple is deliberately stepping away from the OpenAI and Microsoft idea of a "general-purpose" agent that does everything and requires the user to define its behavior with a prompt.</p>

<p>When Apple uses the phrase "<strong>AI for the rest of us</strong>" on its site and in its ads, it is reviving the original campaign from forty years ago that introduced the Macintosh.</p>

<p>
<img src="/posts/del-1-al-15-de-junio-11-de-2024/fe81ad46-2739-4e29-8063-e283b72d28e5_2220x1336.png" alt="">
</p>

<p>

<p>"The rest of us" are the people who do not want complications, people who want to use AI for specific things without configuring anything or writing advanced prompts. All the features are based on generative models, but the whole job of crafting the prompt and communicating with the model is hidden away or reduced to a minimum. We only have to choose a few options in an intuitive way, just as we do when using any app that is not explicitly an AI app. And in those cases where a prompt is needed, such as to generate an image, it is kept to the bare minimum.</p>

<p>This is a key part of Apple's approach: finding concrete things that a generative model can do and that are useful to ordinary users, "the rest of us".</p>

<p>The other key part of Apple's approach is to exploit the fact that it has access to all our information and apps on our phones in order to take the first steps toward turning Siri into a <strong>personal assistant</strong> that can look at our calendar, our emails and messages, and <strong>interact with our apps</strong> on our behalf. For now they have not gone very far and have shown a very simple example: finding out what time your mother's flight arrives. It is a good place to start, and as apps expose more options we will see more advanced examples, provided the on-device LLM that supports Siri is able to manage them correctly.</p>

<p>3. On <strong>June 11</strong>, <strong>François Chollet</strong> (<a href="https://fchollet.com">fchollet.com</a>), the well-known machine learning researcher and author of the excellent book <a href="https://www.manning.com/books/deep-learning-with-python-second-edition">Deep Learning with Python</a>, introduced the ARC Prize (<a href="https://arcprize.org">arcprize.org</a> and <a href="https://x.com/arcprize">X</a>), organized together with <strong>Mike Knoop</strong> (<a href="https://mikeknoop.com">mikeknoop.com</a>). It is a competition with a one-million-dollar prize for solving a problem that LLMs have so far failed to crack. The <strong>Dwarkesh podcast</strong> interview where Chollet and Knoop present the prize is very much worth watching.</p>

<p>The original ARC problem, Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus, was proposed by Chollet himself in a 2019 paper (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01547">On the Measure of Intelligence - arxiv.org</a>) as a way of measuring general intelligence. The challenge is to find patterns in pairs of figures so that you can infer what the next figure should be.</p>

<p>The following illustration shows an example of the figures used in the test. They are drawn on a grid, and there is a pattern that relates the input figure to the output figure. The task is to identify that pattern so that we can determine what the output should be.</p>

<p>
<img src="/posts/del-1-al-15-de-junio-11-de-2024/9a4b2f4c-be66-4736-b28b-f33e642c28a2_1484x636.png" alt="">
</p>

<p>Example figures from the ARC test. Can you identify the pattern and guess what the next figure should be?</p>

<p>Can you spot the pattern in the three examples? What would the resulting figure be?</p>

<p>It is not difficult for humans, who can easily solve 85% of the tasks. The best AI systems, however, do not get past 34%.</p>

<p>In this case, the solutions are the following:</p>

<ul>
<li>
<p>In the pairs on the left, the pattern is: "fill the gap inside the purple squares with a yellow cell". The solution would be four purple squares with yellow cells in the corresponding corners.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>In the middle pairs, the pattern could be expressed as: "move the light-blue cells that sit above the dark-blue ones down to the bottom line". The solution would be a figure with a bottom row of dark-blue cells, except that cell 3 and the last one would be light blue. Above each of them there would be a dark-blue cell.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>In the pairs on the right, the pattern could be described as: "rotate the figure 180 degrees". The solution would be a figure with a first row gray-gray-blue, a second row blue-gray-gray, and a last row blue-blue-blue.</p>
</li>
</ul>

<p>The competition is hosted on Kaggle (<a href="https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2024/">ARC Prize 2024 - kaggle.org</a>), and the prize will go to whichever team manages to build an AI system that matches human performance and reaches 85% accuracy. The submission deadline is November 11. There are already almost 150 teams submitting algorithms (<a href="https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2024/leaderboard">Leaderboard - kaggle.com</a>), and for now the best team is at 38%.</p>

<p>Will someone solve it this year? Will the prize remain unclaimed? Will we be closer to AGI by the end of the year? We will know in a few months.</p>

<p>4. <strong>Swift</strong> is my favorite programming language. It is modern, safe, multiparadigm, expressive, and efficient. Now, in <strong>June 2024</strong>, it has just <strong>turned 10 years old and version 6 has been announced</strong>. A lot has happened since Chris Lattner, its main creator, introduced it on June 2, 2014 at WWDC14.</p>

<p>The following year, toward the end of 2015, <strong>we decided to include Swift in our second-year Computer Engineering course</strong>, <a href="https://moodle2023-24.ua.es/moodle/course/view.php?id=7310">Programming Languages and Paradigms</a>, replacing Scala, which we had been using to explain what a modern language looks like when it integrates the functional programming paradigm with the object-oriented one. Swift had been designed from the beginning with exactly that idea in mind, integrating concepts from functional languages such as Haskell and object-oriented languages such as C# or Objective-C. We also confirmed that Apple had kept Lattner's promise that the language would be open, and that there was a Linux compiler that worked perfectly, so there would be no problem in using it for course assignments.</p>

<p>Within just a few years Swift evolved through several versions (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swift_(programming_language)#History">Swift History - wikipedia.org</a>). Starting with version 4, in 2017, the language stabilized and its basic syntax stopped changing very much. In recent years there have been continuous improvements through the <a href="https://www.swift.org/swift-evolution/">Swift Evolution</a> process. Every change starts as a proposal published on a <a href="https://forums.swift.org/c/evolution/18">public forum</a>, gets discussed and modified, and is eventually accepted or rejected. Accepted proposals are added to the list of approved proposals (<a href="https://github.com/swiftlang/swift-evolution/tree/main/proposals">swift-evolution - github.com</a>) and then implemented in the compiler (<a href="https://github.com/apple/swift">swift - github.com</a>) so they are ready for the next release.</p>

<p>Version 5 of the language was released in 2019 and since then there have been many changes in minor releases. With the regularity of a clock, Apple has shipped two minor versions per year: <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5-released/">5.0</a> and <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.1-released/">5.1</a> in 2019, <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.2-released/">5.2</a> and <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.3-released/">5.3</a> in 2020, <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.4-released/">5.4</a> and <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.5-released/">5.5</a> in 2021, <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.6-released/">5.6</a> and <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.7-released/">5.7</a> in 2022, <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.8-released/">5.8</a> and <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.9-released/">5.9</a> in 2023. This year version <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/swift-5.10-released/">5.10</a> has been released, and at WWDC Apple presented Swift 6 (<a href="https://developer.apple.com/wwdc24/10136">WWDC24: What's new in Swift - apple.com</a>), which will ship in September.</p>

<p>On June 12, the ATP podcast released a very interesting special episode interviewing two Apple engineers responsible for the language and its evolution: <strong>Holly Borla</strong> and <strong>Ben Cohen</strong>.</p>

<p>Today Swift is a very broad and complex language, capable of everything from <a href="https://docs.swift.org/swift-book/documentation/the-swift-programming-language/concurrency/">concurrent programming</a> using actors to programming <a href="https://www.swift.org/blog/embedded-swift-examples/">very low-level microcontrollers</a>. But it is also a language that supports gradual learning. It is very easy to start with, and it could perfectly well serve as the first language in an introductory programming course. All the necessary documentation is in <a href="https://docs.swift.org/swift-book/documentation/the-swift-programming-language/">The Swift Programming Language</a>, starting with the tutorial <a href="https://docs.swift.org/swift-book/documentation/the-swift-programming-language/guidedtour">A Swift Tour</a>.</p>
